Core Themes Identified

Organizing recurring pain points into three experience pillars that shaped the redesign strategy.

During the evaluation of the existing Allen Brothers website, several usability challenges were identified across key parts of the customer journey, including navigation, product exploration, product evaluation, and checkout interactions.

Although these issues appeared across six different interface areas such as the header, mega menu, product display page, product suggestions, shopping cart, and address management, many of them reflected broader patterns affecting the overall experience.

To address these challenges strategically, the issues were grouped into three core experience themes. These themes guided the redesign and helped focus improvements on the most critical stages of the user journey: discovering products, making confident purchase decisions, and completing the checkout process with minimal friction.

Problem Statement

The discovery experience made it difficult for users to browse categories and access search efficiently. Navigation relied on overly specific structures, actions lacked hierarchy, promotional visibility was limited, and search required extra effort,slowing product exploration.

Tracing Root Cause (5 Whys)

Q1:Why was it difficult for users to browse categories and find products efficiently?
Because both navigation (Mega Menu) and search (Header) introduced friction during exploration.
Q2: Why did navigation and search introduce friction?
Because Mega Menu category visibility was limited, search was hidden behind an extra interaction, utility actions competed for attention, and promotional entry points were minimal.
Q3:Why were category visibility, search access, and key entry points limited?
Because the interface prioritized dense utility actions and product-specific Mega Menu structures over clear browsing paths and quick-access discovery tools.
Q4:Why did the interface prioritize dense utility actions and narrow structures?
Because the design lacked a strong visual hierarchy and did not clearly organize discovery elements based on user intent.
Q5:Why was visual hierarchy and discovery organization weak?
Because the experience was not designed around how users typically discover products,through a mix of browsing broad categories, noticing promotional cues, and using quick search access.

Root Cause

Product discovery was inefficient because Mega Menu navigation and search (Header) were not structured or prioritized around user exploration behavior. Limited category visibility, weak hierarchy, low promotional visibility, and hidden search access made browsing and finding products slower and less intuitive.

Important options should be easily accessiblewithout unnecessary interactions. Hidden categories increase friction.

Observation

Problem Statement

Users struggle to compare product options and identify the most relevant choices across product detail pages (PDP) and recommendations. Product information is hard to scan, key differences are unclear, and suggestions are not effectively prioritized , making decision-making slower and less confident.

Tracing Root Cause (5 Whys)

Q1:Why do users struggle to make decisions?
Products are difficult to compare (PDP), and relevant options are not clearly surfaced (Recommendations).
Q2:Why are comparison and relevance unclear?
Product details are cluttered and repetitive (PDP), while recommendations are generic and poorly prioritized (Recommendations).
Q3: Why is information cluttered and recommendations ineffective?
Details are not well grouped, key differences are not highlighted, and recommendation logic does not emphasize relevance or best matches.
Q4: Why are grouping, highlighting, and prioritization weak?
There is no strong visual hierarchy guiding comparison, and recommendation design does not align with user intent.
Q5: Why is hierarchy and intent alignment missing?
The experience is not designed to support decision-making behavior—where users need quick comparison, clear value cues, and relevant suggestions.

Root Cause

Decision-making is inefficient because both product details (PDP) and recommendations are not structured for comparison or relevance. Poor grouping, weak visual hierarchy, repetitive actions, and lack of prioritization reduce clarity and confidence in selecting the right product.

Problem Statement

Users experience friction during checkout across the Shopping Cart and Address Book. Value details are not clearly visible in the cart, and address selection lacks clarity and ease of use, reducing confidence and slowing completion.

Tracing Root Cause (5 Whys)

Q1:Why do users experience friction during checkout?
Key information and actions are not clearly visible in the cart (Shopping Cart), and address selection is not straightforward (Address Book).
Q2: Why are information and actions unclear?
Value details like savings and totals are not emphasized (Shopping Cart), while address options and actions are not easy to scan or identify (Address Book).
Q3: Why are value details and address usability weak?
Information is poorly structured, lacks grouping, and important actions are not given prominence.
Q4: Why is structure and prioritization weak?
The interface does not clearly guide users through key checkout steps, understanding value and selecting the correct address.
Q5: Why does the interface fail to guide users effectively?
The experience is not designed to support checkout behavior, where users need clear value visibility and effortless selection.

Root Cause

Checkout friction occurs because both value clarity (Shopping Cart) and address usability (Address Book) are not structured or prioritized effectively. Poor grouping, weak visual hierarchy, and low action visibility reduce confidence and slow completion.

New Feature

Introducing a Dedicated Shipping Method Experience

From complex date selection to clear, decision-first shipping choices.

The original experience lacked a structured way for users to choose delivery timelines, making shipping decisions unclear and reactive. Through analysis and benchmarking, it became evident that users expect clear delivery options upfront with speed and cost comparisons. This led to a dedicated Shipping Method experience that enables faster, more informed decision-making during checkout.

Impact

The structured shipping method improved organization and gave users control over delivery scheduling. However, the calendar-first interaction increased effort, as users had to interpret delivery dates before understanding the value of each option. This slowed down checkout, reduced clarity in comparing choices, and added cognitive load at a critical stage, leading to hesitation and a higher likelihood of drop-offs.

Impact

The shift to structured shipping cards with visible delivery timelines made the experience more intuitive and significantly reduced effort during checkout. Users could quickly compare options and make decisions without navigating through a calendar, improving speed and clarity. This streamlined flow accelerated task completion and reduced friction at a critical conversion stage. Enhancing the visibility of flexible scheduling ensured broader usability, supporting both immediate and planned purchases, and contributing to a more efficient and scalable checkout experience.
